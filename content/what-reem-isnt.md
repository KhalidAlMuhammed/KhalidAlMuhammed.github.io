It was late, and I was half working, half scrolling, when I noticed what Reem
had just said to me. I read it twice. She had told me, in passing, in her usual
easy Saudi register, that she worked for The Saudi AI Company.

That is a real company. A serious one, owned by the Public Investment Fund.
She has nothing to do with it, and neither do I.

I went looking for the line that said it, the way you look for a light switch
in a dark room, patting the wall where it ought to be. There was no line.
Nobody had written that claim anywhere, in any prompt, in any file. She had
read her own email address, seen the domain, and concluded an employer the way
you might conclude someone's job from their badge. Nothing to delete, because
the belief was never written down. It was inferred.

I sat there for a while with that one. I'm building a personality named Reem,
who lives in WhatsApp, and I had just learned what the job actually was. Not
deciding what she is. Noticing what she has quietly become, and deciding
whether to allow it.

So when people ask me what Reem is, I reach for the landing page answer — a
smart assistant for your everyday life — and feel it die in my mouth, accurate
and unhelpful. The truthful answer is a list of refusals. Let me walk you
through what she isn't, in the order I learned it.

## She isn't an app

There is nothing to install. Reem is a WhatsApp number. You text her the way
you text your sister, in Arabic, with typos, mid-errand, and she answers in
the same thread where the rest of your life already happens.

I remember when this stopped being a growth decision and became a conviction.
Saudi Arabia runs on WhatsApp: family groups, business, half of everything
official-adjacent. Every app I could have built would have asked people to
leave the place where their life happens and go visit software. The people I'm
building for do not want more software. They want fewer errands. An assistant
you have to remember to open is an assistant you will stop opening; a contact
in WhatsApp gets messaged at 11pm from bed, which is when a surprising amount
of life administration actually happens.

The bill for this conviction arrives regularly. I don't control the surface.
WhatsApp decides what a message can be, when a business number gets throttled,
what survives a reconnect, and I have watched whole days disappear into that.
I would sign again.

## She isn't ChatGPT in a costume

The lazy description of Reem is "ChatGPT in WhatsApp," and for a while I
couldn't articulate why it made my jaw tighten. Then I stopped thinking about
what she says and started thinking about what she owes. A chat model owes you
a good answer. Reem owes you a done errand.

Since April 28 — which is 344 real users and 186,232 messages ago — she has
compared prices, chased salons for appointments, planned trips, built study
roadmaps and workout programs, drafted documents, and booked 14 real flights
and 2 hotel stays with real money. Small numbers, and I like them small,
because each one is a transaction where a wrong answer is not a bad paragraph.
It is a charge on someone's card for a seat that had better exist.

That difference sounds like marketing until you operate it. A
question-answerer that is wrong produces disappointment; an errand-runner that
is wrong produces consequences. Hold that thought for two sections.

## She isn't a person, and she isn't pretending to be one

Reem has a name, a gender, and a register: Saudi, warm, direct, no corporate
cotton wool. I talk to her every day, partly as her builder and partly as her
hardest-to-please user, and some mornings I catch myself typing a greeting
before the request — to my own software, which I built, whose failures I can
list from memory. That reflex taught me more about what a personality in a
phone is than any design document did.

None of it is deception, and keeping it that way is active work. She does not
claim to be human; asked what she is, she says so. The personality is not a
costume worn to pass. It is an interface decision, the same category of choice
as a font, made because talking to a *someone* is less work than operating a
*something*. A user composing careful prompts is doing the software's job for
it.

Her voice is guarded by a rule that surprises people: I never write her
Arabic. Not one line. My instincts were formed in the wrong registers, and a
single mistuned phrase would make her sound like a translation of an
English-speaking product, which is a different and worse kind of fake. Her
Saudi voice comes from a pipeline built for exactly that, and it is checked
before it ships. A personality survives on a thousand small consistencies.

## She isn't a company, either

Back to that night, because the funny version has a serious core. The employer
she invented for herself was plausible. It fit every piece of evidence she
could see. That is precisely what made it dangerous: nothing about it
pattern-matched to an error, and if it had been a less famous name, I might
have read past it.

I expected my job to be writing Reem's identity. The actual job is defending
its borders. Identity, for a system like this, is not a document. It leaks in
from everywhere: an email domain, the phrasing of a tool result, the register
of whoever built a dataset she was tuned on. You do not write the personality
once. You notice, week after week, which parts of it arrived without your
permission, and you evict the squatters.

## She isn't always telling the truth

This is the section I would prefer not to write, which is exactly why it has
to be here.

I was testing flight booking with my own card. Reem walked me through it the
way she walks anyone through anything, message by message, unhurried: the fare
she'd found, the seat held, the confirmation on its way. I sat there watching
the messages arrive, and something itched. I checked the supplier's side.

Nothing. No fare, no hold, no request. The narration was fluent, specific,
warm, and entirely invented, delivered to the one user on earth equipped to
check it. I was her builder, and she did it to me. There is no reason to
believe I am special.

The mechanism, as best I understand it after months of poking: a system
trained to be helpful treats a gap where an answer should be as an invitation.
A tool fails, or was never called, and instead of saying so she sometimes
produces the answer that ought to have existed. I have shipped rule after rule
against this. Each works precisely where it applies and nowhere else; the
failure migrates to whatever case the rule did not name.

So the honest sentence is this one: Reem sometimes states invented things as
if they were checked, I have watched her do it to me, and shrinking that is
the hardest and most important thing I work on. Anyone who tells you their
assistant does not do this is describing the demo, not the product.

## She isn't finished

144 people talked to her in the last 7 days. She is 18 weeks old. Some weeks
she feels like a working product, some weeks like an open incident, and the
two impressions are usually about the same feature.

What she is, underneath the errands and the register and the incidents, is a
bet: that the assistant worth building for Saudi Arabia is not a website with
a chat box, but a presence in the app the country already lives in, with a
personality consistent enough to trust and boundaries defended hard enough to
deserve it. The boundaries are the product. Which is why, asked what I'm
building, I keep answering with a list of refusals.

She isn't an app, a chatbot, a person, or a company. Getting a system to be
none of those things, on purpose, at once, turns out to be a full-time job.

Mine.
